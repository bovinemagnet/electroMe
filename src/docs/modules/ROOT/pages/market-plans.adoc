= Market plans
:description: Harvesting every published retail plan for your network and comparing it against your usage.

Defining tariffs by hand answers "is my plan better than that one". Harvesting the market answers
the question you actually have: *where does my plan rank against everything I could switch to?*

== Where the data comes from

Australian energy retailers are obliged to publish their plans as machine-readable Product
Reference Data under the Consumer Data Right. electroMe reads it directly from the register, which
is public, free and needs no key.

Despite the branding, the endpoint serves Victorian plans as well as the rest of the National
Electricity Market: Victorian offers carry a `@VEC` suffix on their identifier.

Victorian Energy Compare has an undocumented API for the same underlying data. electroMe does not
use it, because its terms restrict access to a browser for your own household.

== Running a harvest

Press *Fetch market plans* at the bottom of the page.

Nothing is fetched when the application starts. A harvest reaches a public regulator's
infrastructure and takes tens of seconds, so it happens only when you ask, and the application
remains usable with no network at all.

A typical harvest of the AusNet network:

[cols="3,1"]
|===
| Retailers queried | 80
| Plans published across all networks | 16,186
| Plan responses cached for this network | 584
| Mapped successfully | 574
| Skipped, and named in the summary | 10
| Network coverage | 98%
| Distinct tariffs after removing duplicates | under 200
| Time taken | under 30 seconds
|===

Responses are cached on disk and refetched only when the published plan changes, so a second
harvest is close to instant.

Not every published plan can be mapped. A plan whose rates cannot be read with confidence is
refused rather than guessed at, and the harvest summary names every reason a plan was skipped, each
with a count — not just the first few. A truncated list of gaps reads as a complete list of gaps,
and refusing a plan is only better than mispricing it if you can see that it happened. The same
count and reasons reappear beside the answer on xref:browsing-plans.adoc[the plan browser],
because one of the plans refused there could have been the cheapest of all.

Coverage is reported as one percentage: the share of this network's plans the tariff model could
hold. A mapper that quietly reads a capped free-energy window as unlimited cheap power reports full
coverage; one that refuses it reports less, and the lower figure is the honest one. Reading an
earlier version's mapping of this same cache reported 583 mapped and 1 failed — full marks, and
wrong. The ten now refused are six plans with a seasonal demand charge the model has no season for,
three plans where a single capped allowance is shared across windows that do not adjoin, and one
malformed response.

[#duplicates]
== Why deduplication matters

A single retailer publishes thousands of plan identifiers, because every postcode group and meter
variant is a separate plan even when the rates are identical. Six hundred plans collapse to under
two hundred distinct tariffs.

Plans with identical charges from the *same* retailer are collapsed to one. Two different
retailers at the same price are both kept — that is a real choice, not duplication.

[#requirements]
== Plans with requirements

Some of the cheapest published plans are only offered to households that own or have joined
something: solar panels, a home battery, an electric vehicle, a loyalty programme, a streaming
bundle. Those are marked *requirements* in the comparison; hover the marker to read them.

This matters more than it sounds. On a real harvest the cheapest plan overall required solar, a
battery *and* an electric vehicle. Presented without distinction it would have promised a saving
the household simply could not take.

[NOTE]
====
Ordinary conditions that nearly every plan carries — being in the right distribution zone, having
an applicable network tariff, having a smart meter — are *not* marked. Flagging those would mark
almost every plan and tell you nothing.

The published data gives no structured way to tell the two apart: every eligibility entry is typed
`OTHER`, so the distinction is drawn from the wording. Treat the marker as a prompt to read the
plan's own terms, not as a guarantee either way.
====

== Charges read from a harvested plan

Every charge type electroMe understands can come from a harvested plan, not only a hand-written
one: capped and tiered time-of-use windows, demand charges, discounts and a controlled-load rate
for a hot water circuit. See xref:tariff-reference.adoc[Tariff reference] for what each one means
and how it prices your usage.

A published time-of-use window is sometimes capped — free or heavily discounted for the first
several kilowatt hours of the day, at the ordinary rate after — and sometimes split at midnight,
because a time of day cannot say "until 11:00 tomorrow". Both are read as the single tariff they
represent: a capped window keeps its one allowance regardless of how many rate rows or window
entries the register used to publish it, and a window split at midnight is rejoined into the one
overnight band it describes. Where a plan publishes one allowance shared across windows that do not
adjoin, sharing the cap between two separate bands would hand the household the allowance twice —
GloBird's EasyEV plan does this, capping the sum of an overnight and an afternoon window together —
so the plan is refused rather than approximated.

Demand charges, a controlled-load rate and proportional discounts are also read from the contract
now. Reading only the first tariff period previously left a demand plan priced as an ordinary flat
tariff with its largest single charge missing, and a household with a controlled circuit had that
energy priced at the ordinary rate on every harvested plan. A demand charge is only mapped where it
does not vary by season: every demand plan currently on the AusNet register publishes a summer rate
and a winter one, and charging one of them all year is a different tariff rather than an
approximation, so a seasonal demand charge is refused — see below for what discounts are and are
not costed.

== What is not modelled

Discounts and credits::
A discount published as a percentage off the whole bill is costed, with the retailer's own wording
about what it requires recorded alongside — see xref:tariff-reference.adoc#discount[the discount
charge type] for how a conditional total is marked. A fixed-amount, one-off sign-up credit is
deliberately not costed: the model applies a fixed discount once per costing, so pricing a sign-up
credit that way would subtract it from every year the household stays rather than only the first.
Those are shown beside the plan instead, alongside fees — see
xref:browsing-plans.adoc#fees[Fees and incentives].

Fees::
Membership, disconnection and payment-processing fees are not costed. They *are* read and shown
on a plan's detail panel, labelled as excluded — see
xref:browsing-plans.adoc#fees[Fees and incentives]. Costing them would mix GST bases, because fee
amounts are published inclusive while unit prices are exclusive.

Seasonal tariffs::
Where a plan publishes several seasonal usage periods, the first is used — the tariff model has no
seasonal dimension, and mapping one period is honest where blending them would not be. A seasonal
*demand* charge is refused instead of picked from; see above.

Price changes::
Rates are what is published today. The feed gives no advance notice of changes.

== Your own plans always win

A plan file in `plans/` takes precedence over a harvested plan with the same identifier. Your
hand-written tariff is a deliberate statement — usually the plan you are actually on, which may no
longer be published — and is never silently replaced.
