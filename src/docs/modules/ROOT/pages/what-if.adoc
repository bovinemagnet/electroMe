= What-if modelling
:description: What shifting load, adding solar, or adding a battery would be worth per year.

The comparison answers "which plan suits how I use power now". This panel answers the more useful
question: *what would change the answer?*

== Two different questions

This page covers two things that look similar and are not:

*Scenarios* rearrange energy you already use — shifting load, adding solar, adding a battery.
They are on the dashboard.

*Appliances* add load that is not in your data at all, and come with a second question: when
should it run? That is the xref:what-if.adoc#appliances[appliance screen] at `/what-if`.

[#appliances]
== Adding an appliance

An electric vehicle, a pool pump, a hot water service, more air conditioning. Pick one, adjust the
figures, and the screen answers three questions: what it would cost a year, when to run it, and
whether owning it changes which plan you should be on.

The third is the interesting one. Adding eleven kilowatt hours a night to a household whose tariff
prices midnight to six at 4.99c is not a uniform increase — it lands almost entirely in the
cheapest window, which makes that window's rate worth far more than it is today. A plan that loses
on your current consumption can win comfortably once there is a car in the household.

=== Presets, in units you already know

Nobody knows their pool pump's draw, so every appliance arrives with real defaults and every field
stays editable.

The electric vehicle is described the way a driver thinks: *kilometres a week* and *kilowatt hours
per hundred kilometres*. You know your weekly mileage; you do not know your nightly
kilowatt-hours, so the conversion happens for you.

=== Two kinds of load

*Schedulable* — a block of energy that must fit inside a window. You say how much and by when; the
software says at what time. An electric vehicle, a pool pump, hot water.

A charger can pause and resume, so it takes the cheapest half hours wherever they fall. A pool
pump cannot, so it takes the cheapest unbroken stretch. Windows that run past midnight are normal
and are handled as such.

*Shaped* — weather-driven load whose timing is not a choice. Air conditioning. There is no run
time to recommend, because you cool the house when it is hot, and offering one would be advice
nobody can take.

=== When should it run

The recommendation is a specific clock time with a cost attached, and the cost of the obvious
alternative — plugging in and charging straight away — beside it. The gap between the two is what
setting a timer is worth.

Every plan gets *its own* schedule. Sharing one would understate every plan whose cheap window
sits somewhere else, which is precisely the effect being measured.

Where a plan has no time structure at all — a flat rate, or a pure block tariff — the screen says
*timing makes no difference on this plan* rather than reporting an arbitrary half hour as though
it had been chosen.

[#scheduling-method]
=== How the time is chosen, and where that is exact

The cost of adding a kilowatt hour in a given half hour is simply the tariff's rate in that half
hour. So finding the best time is a scan over forty-eight numbers rather than forty-eight
re-costings of the whole year — which is what makes optimising against every plan at once
affordable.

That shortcut is exact for flat and time-of-use tariffs. It is not exact for:

* *Block tariffs*, where what a unit costs depends on how much you have already used in the
  period.
* *Demand charges*, where added load costs nothing or a great deal depending on whether it lifts
  your monthly peak.

So the discipline is: **choose the time by marginal rate, then cost that schedule in full**. The
cost shown is always exact. Only the chosen time can be slightly off, and only on those two kinds
of plan — which say so, on themselves, rather than the whole screen carrying a disclaimer it does
not need.

=== Solar changes the answer

If you already export at midday, the cost of consuming then is not the import rate — it is the
feed-in rate you forgo, typically a few cents against forty or fifty.

So the recommendation moves from overnight to the middle of the day on its own once there is solar
in your data. That is why "charge at midday" is right for a solar household and wrong without one.

It moves only if the appliance is *available* then. A car plugged in from 18:00 to 07:00 is at
work at midday, and the window constrains the choice before the rates do.

=== If it will not fit

Eleven kilowatt hours at two kilowatts needs five and a half hours. A four-hour window cannot
deliver it, and the screen says so — naming the shortfall and the earliest deadline that would
work — rather than quietly charging a shorter session. A price for energy never delivered would be
worse than the message.

[#controlled-load]
=== Hot water and the controlled circuit

Victorian households commonly have hot water on a *separate controlled circuit* with its own,
cheaper tariff. The retailer energises it for a stated window and prices what it draws well below
the ordinary rate.

Pricing that energy as ordinary consumption would misprice it by the whole gap between two
tariffs, so it is modelled properly: a `controlledLoad` charge on a plan, and a third series
alongside consumption and export in your data. Your interval file is read for it automatically —
retailers label the register inconsistently, so the match is on wording rather than an agreed
code.

Two consequences worth knowing:

* A plan that offers *no* controlled rate still charges for that energy, at its ordinary rate.
  Leaving it unpriced would make every such plan look artificially cheap.
* Energy falling outside the energised window is likewise priced as ordinary consumption, because
  that is what the retailer does.

[#appliance-assumptions]
=== What the appliance figures assume

* *Runs are spread evenly across the week.* Five charges land on five of every seven days. Nobody
  drives to a schedule, so treat it as an average rather than a plan.
* *The appliance runs at full power until it is done*, which is what a timer or a charging app
  actually does — not a continuous optimum nobody achieves. This is the same discipline as the
  battery controller.
* *Air conditioning is an increment.* Your consumption is already strongly summer-peaked, so some
  cooling is present in the data. The preset models using *more*, not adding it from nothing.
* *A demand tariff is sensitive to the schedule.* The costing is exact, but adding a car to a
  demand plan can lift your monthly peak sharply, and the cost then depends heavily on when it
  runs. The plan is marked as an estimate for that reason.

=== More than one appliance at once

The form on the screen asks about one appliance, but the modelling underneath it is not limited
to that: it can schedule and cost several appliances together. A household with two electric
vehicles does not have one vehicle twice — they want different amounts of energy, are plugged in
at different times, and are charged on different nights. Each is scheduled on its own terms
against each plan's own rates, and what they cost together is then a single full costing of the
series with all of them present, which is why the combined figure is exact even though each
appliance's schedule was chosen independently of the others.

That independence is the one approximation left, and it only bites where one appliance changes
what the next one pays — a capped window, a block rate, a demand charge. Those are exactly the
plans <<scheduling-method,already marked as inexact>> for the single-appliance case, and the same
note applies for the same reason once a second appliance is added.

== How a scenario is scored

Each scenario transforms your consumption, then re-costs the result *against every plan you have
defined*, and reports the saving against your current cheapest option.

The scoring is deliberately against the best plan available *under that scenario*, not against the
plan you are on today. Part of a battery's value is that it changes which tariff suits you, and
holding the tariff fixed would understate it. Where a scenario would move you to a different plan,
the table says so.

Savings are scaled to a year from whatever window you have selected, so a three-month window does
not read as an annual figure.

== The scenarios

Shift load out of the peak::
Moves a proportion of the energy you use in the dearest window into the cheapest one, day by day,
spread evenly. This models running the dishwasher, the pool pump or the car charger overnight
instead of at dinner time. Energy is conserved exactly — nothing is created or destroyed, only
moved.

Add solar::
Models a north-facing array and nets its output against your consumption *within each half hour*.
Only surplus is exported, only shortfall imported. That granularity matters enormously: netting at
a daily level would treat a midday surplus and an evening import as cancelling out, when the
tariff does the opposite — evening imports cost around 50 cents a unit while exports earn a few.

Add a battery::
Charges in the tariff's cheapest window and discharges in its dearest, bounded by capacity, power
rating and round-trip efficiency. Any solar surplus charges the battery before being exported.

Both together::
Solar applied first, then the battery, because a battery should work against consumption that
solar has already offset.

Adjust the array size, battery capacity and shift proportion with the controls; the figures
re-compute.

== Reading the result

Expect solar and a battery *together* to save less than the two figures added up. They compete for
the same evening consumption — once solar has cut your daytime import, there is less left for the
battery to displace.

Expect total grid *import* to rise when a battery is added, even as the bill falls. Storing energy
costs energy: round-trip losses are real, and the model does not hide them.

[#assumptions]
== The assumptions these rest on

Read every scenario figure as an *upper bound*.

There is no weather in the solar model::
Output is what a cloudless sky would deliver. Annual generation therefore comes out roughly a fifth
above what a real array in the same place yields. The *shape* is right — generation peaks near
solar noon and has largely finished before the evening peak window opens — and shape is what a
tariff comparison needs.

The battery controller is deliberately simple::
It charges cheap and discharges dear on fixed windows. It is not a perfect-foresight optimiser,
because an optimiser would report a saving no real installation achieves.

One consequence, which is a genuine limitation rather than a defect: with both solar and a
grid-charge window, the battery fills overnight and has no headroom left when the midday surplus
arrives, so that surplus is exported rather than stored. Real units avoid this by reserving
capacity against a solar forecast. A rule-based controller has no forecast.

Capital cost is not modelled::
The figures are annual bill savings. Whether an installation pays for itself is arithmetic you
must do against a real quote.
