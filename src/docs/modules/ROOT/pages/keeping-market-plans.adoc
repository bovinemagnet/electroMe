= Keeping market plans
:description: Choosing which published tariffs are worth keeping as plan files, and how your own files are protected when you do.

xref:browsing-plans.adoc[The plan browser] answers what a tariff would cost this household. This
screen answers the question before it: which published tariffs are worth having as files at all.
It shows what each published plan charges and lets you tick the ones to keep.

Open it from the navigation, labelled *Market*, between *Plans* and *Rates*.

The screen costs every published plan against the household's own half-hourly consumption over the
selected window, and the totals column is the default sort. It prices each plan _as the register
publishes it today_, never from a copy already saved — a screen whose job is to show you that your
saved copy has gone out of date cannot then price the out-of-date copy beside the warning. (The
plan browser, by contrast, lets a local file win, which is right there: a hand-written plan is a
deliberate statement.)

Also note: with no interval data loaded, the totals column is simply absent and the screen still
lists what every plan charges.

== Reading the register

Press *Read the register*.

Nothing is read at startup. A read reaches a public regulator's infrastructure, so it happens only
when you ask, and the application stays usable with no network at all.

Reading fills the on-disk harvest cache — it does not touch the plans directory. Nothing is
written to a file until you say so. Before it writes anything, the screen names the absolute plans
directory it would write into, so you know exactly where a save would land before you commit to
one.

== The table

Each row is one published plan, from the register or the cache behind it.

Tick box::
Marks the plan for saving.

Plan::
The plan's name, with the file name it would be saved as underneath it. Beside the name, chips
mark eligibility requirements and, where the plan publishes any, "N fees not costed" — see
<<fees>>.

Retailer::
Who publishes the plan.

Shape::
Flat, time-of-use, block or demand, the same vocabulary xref:browsing-plans.adoc[the plan browser]
and xref:plan-rates.adoc[plan rates] use.

Rate columns::
One column per published rate component — daily supply, evening peak, middle of day,
overnight/off-peak, shoulder, flat, solar feed-in, and so on — but only for the components some
plan on the page actually charges; a network with no demand plans on it gets no demand column.
Rates are in cents and GST inclusive. A dash means the plan has no such charge; an asterisk means
the rate holds only up to a daily or monthly allowance.

=== Filters

Search::
Matches the plan name or the retailer, ignoring case.

Retailer::
Narrows the table to one retailer.

Shape::
Any, flat, time of use, block or demand.

Sort by::
What it would cost you (the default), daily supply, evening peak rate, overnight/off-peak, middle
of day, shoulder, flat rate, solar feed-in, retailer or name.

Show::
25, 50, 100 or all rows.

Hide plans I already have::
Drops every row already <<status,held>>, so the table shows only what would actually be new.

Allow flexible / market rates::
A tick box, off by default. Lets in the plans whose rates follow the wholesale market rather than
a published figure. See the new section for why they are held back.

Every criterion travels in the query string, so the URL fully describes the screen: it can be
bookmarked, sent to somebody else, and the back button returns you to exactly the view you left.

== Reading the colour

There are two colour encodings in the table, and they are deliberately different kinds.

*The rate cells* are shaded neutrally — grey, no hue — by where each rate sits within *its own
column*, lightest for the cheapest quarter and darkest for the dearest. Per column, because a daily
supply charge in cents per day and a peak rate in cents per kilowatt hour are different units; one
scale across both would only ever say that the supply charge is the expensive part of every plan.
Ranked by position rather than by distance, so a single outlier priced at four times the market
cannot flatten every other plan into one shade. Solar feed-in inverts, because there the largest
number is the best one.

The rate cells carry no hue because the band colours already mean something fixed everywhere else
in electroMe — red is the evening peak window, amber the middle of the day, green the cheapest
window — and tinting an "Evening peak" cell red for being expensive would give red two jobs in one
table.

*The totals column* is the only coloured thing in a row, and so is where the eye lands. It uses a
diverging scale: green for cheaper than the plan you are on, red for dearer, with the middle of the
scale anchored on your own tariff — the one named by `electrome.baseline.plan` (see
xref:configuration.adoc[Configuration]). Anchored there rather than on the cheapest row because
"cheaper than what I pay now" is the decision being made, and because a scale anchored on the
cheapest row would move every time you changed a filter.

Each row also carries a bar whose length is proportional to the gap, the widest gap on the page
filling its half of the track. Magnitude is carried by length as well as by colour, so the ranking
still reads in greyscale and for anyone who cannot separate the two hues.

A legend above the table names what the middle of the scale is and what the widest gap on the page
is worth.

A plan can shade light on one rate column and still cost you more overall — a cheap evening rate
with a heavy daily supply charge is the common case — which is why the total is the column to read
and the rate shading is context rather than a verdict.

[#status]
== "In your plans directory"

A column comparing each published plan against the plans directory, in one of three states.

Not held::
Saving this plan would write a new file.

Held::
You already have this plan, and your file matches what is currently published.

Rates moved::
You have this plan, but the retailer now publishes something different from what your file holds.
Saving updates your file to match.

The third state is the one that matters. A saved plan quietly going out of date is invisible
everywhere else in the application — the dashboard, the plan browser and the rates table all read
your file as though it were still current, because nothing about a stale file looks wrong from the
outside. This column is the only place that mismatch is ever shown.

== Saving

Tick the plans you want and press *Save the ticked plans*. Each becomes an ordinary YAML file in
the plans directory — the same hand-editable format documented in
xref:defining-a-plan.adoc[Defining a plan].

After saving, the plans directory is re-read immediately, so the plans you have just kept appear on
the dashboard, in the plan browser and in the rates table straight away, with no restart and no
separate *Reload plan files* step.

The save report lists every file by name, and what happened to it:

* *New file* — nothing with this identifier existed, so a file was written.
* *Replaced* — a file electroMe had written for this plan already existed, and its rates had
  moved, so it was overwritten with the current ones.
* *Already up to date* — a file electroMe had written for this plan already existed and matched, so
  nothing changed.
* *New file, renamed* — see <<protection>>.

[#protection]
== How your own files are protected

This is the part worth reading carefully before you tick anything.

A file electroMe writes carries a `source:` block naming the published plan identifier and the day
the register was read:

[source,yaml]
----
source:
  cdrPlanId: "GLO785330MR@VEC"
  readOn: 2026-08-21
----

The rule that follows from it:

* A file carrying a `source:` block was written by electroMe, and *may* be replaced when the
  register publishes something different for that identifier.
* A file without one was written by hand, and is *never* overwritten — no exceptions.

If a hand-written file already occupies the name a harvested plan would use, the harvested plan
does not touch it. It takes the next free name instead, and the save report says so as
*New file, renamed*. Nothing you wrote by hand is ever silently replaced because a retailer
happened to publish something under a similar name.

Plans are matched by their published identifier, not by file name. Rename a saved file by hand, and
the next save still recognises it as the same plan and updates it in place, rather than writing a
second copy alongside it.

Deleting the `source:` block from a file makes it yours. From that point electroMe treats it as
hand-written and leaves it alone, exactly as if you had typed it from scratch.

Writes go through a temporary file and an atomic move, so the plan loader — which reads the
directory independently, at startup and on *Reload plan files* — never sees a half-written file.

== What is written

Rates are written exactly as harvested, with `gstInclusive: true`.

This is deliberate rather than a shortcut. Harvested rates have already been grossed up to include
GST; dividing them back down to an exclusive figure would turn an exact published rate into a
repeating decimal, and reading that number back in would show it as something other than what was
actually published.

Rate values are written as quoted YAML scalars — `"22.60"` rather than `22.60` — because unquoted
YAML reads a number like that as a float and drops the trailing zero. An unquoted `22.60` becomes
`22.6` the moment it is written, which would make every saved plan look, on the very next read, as
though its rate had moved when nothing about it had.

[#market-linked]
== Flexible and market-linked rates

A small number of retailers sell exposure to the wholesale electricity market rather than a rate.
You are billed at what the energy actually costs in the market at the time you use it, usually with
a membership fee on top. On the AusNet network this is Amber Electric and Flow Power — about thirty
published plans between them.

These plans still publish a unit price. They have to: the register models a tariff as a set of
rates and has nowhere to record "it depends". Amber, for example, publishes 40.2c per kilowatt hour
and a daily supply charge of 53.8c, and then states in the plan's own price-variation text that the
rate is illustrative only. Costing a year of the household's consumption against that figure
produces a total that looks exactly like every other total in the table and means nothing.

That illustrative supply charge — 59.2c including GST — is the lowest published anywhere on the
AusNet network; the next lowest is 71.5c. So sorted by daily supply the plan comes top of the
table, and its supply cell shades as the cheapest in that column. Its illustrative usage rate,
though, is a flat 44.2c per kilowatt hour, which is high, so its total lands near the bottom.
Neither position means anything. The plan tops one column and props up another, and both figures
come from a rate the retailer has said is illustrative. The danger is not that these plans rank
well or badly; it is that they rank at all, on a scale they do not belong to.

So they are left out of the table by default, and the tick box *Allow flexible / market rates*
brings them in. When shown, three things mark them: a "Market-linked rates" chip on the row whose
tooltip carries the retailer's own wording verbatim, an asterisk on the total, and a standing note
above the table. The count line says how many are being held back and offers to show them.

=== How they are recognised

The register carries no flag for this, so electroMe reads what the retailer says in the plan's
price-variation text and looks for a claim that the price follows the wholesale or spot market. The
test is deliberately narrow. "Prices are subject to change" is not enough — nearly every plan in the
register says that, and matching it would hide the whole market. Neither is "pass through", which a
dozen ordinary plans use for network charges recovered at cost. Naming the wholesale or spot market
is the claim that actually distinguishes them, and across the AusNet network it selects exactly the
two retailers that sell such plans and nothing else.

Because this reads free text rather than a published flag, a retailer who words it differently could
be missed. The chip carries the retailer's own sentence so the classification can be judged rather
than taken on trust, and a plan you can see is market-linked but which is not marked is worth
reporting.

[#fees]
== Fees and incentives

Fees and incentives are shown as chips on the row, the same as on
xref:browsing-plans.adoc#fees[the plan browser], and, with one exception, none of them is included
in any figure on this screen.

The exception is a mandatory membership fee — one you must pay to be on the plan at all. That fee
is costed into the total, because it is unavoidable, and the row carries a chip stating so with the
yearly amount. See xref:tariff-reference.adoc#membership[membership].

Every other published fee stays out of every figure, and this is deliberate rather than a gap: a
late payment fee, a paper bill fee, a card processing fee, a disconnection fee are all facts about
what the household does rather than about the tariff, and costing them would charge a reader for
behaviour they have not got.

The chip is there so a plan carrying a fee cannot be saved, and later ranked, without you knowing
the fee exists.
