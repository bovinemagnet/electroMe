= Reading the dashboard
:description: What each panel and figure on the electroMe dashboard means.

The page is ordered as an argument: the answer first, then the evidence for it.

== The verdict

The headline figure is what the *cheapest plan you have defined* would have cost over the selected
window, on your actual consumption. Beside it sits the gap to the dearest plan — the size of the
decision you are making.

The window defaults to the most recent twelve months of data you have. Change it with the date
controls; everything below re-costs.

== The stat tiles

Consumed::
Total energy drawn from the grid over the window, and the daily average.

Effective rate::
The entire bill divided by the energy used, in cents per kilowatt hour. This is the number to
compare between plans, because it folds in the daily supply charge. A plan with a low usage rate
and a high supply charge can easily lose to one advertised as dearer.

Share of bill from the peak window::
The proportion of the bill attributable to the dearest time-of-use band, alongside the proportion
of *energy* consumed in it. When the first is much larger than the second, timing is costing you
money. This tile is omitted when the cheapest plan is a flat rate, because the figure would be
meaningless rather than merely zero.

Average draw at the daily peak::
Your highest average power draw across the day, and when it occurs. This is the figure a demand
tariff would price.

== What each plan would have cost

Each row is one tariff, cheapest first. The bar is the bill broken into its charge components and
scaled so that rows share one axis — so you are comparing like with like across plans, not just
totals.

Colours mean the same thing everywhere on the page:

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Red | The dearest usage window
| Amber | The middle-of-day window, where a solar-soak rate usually sits
| Blue | Shoulder rates
| Green | The cheapest window
| Grey | Daily supply charge
|===

The daytime window keeps its amber whether or not it happens to be the cheapest rate on that
particular plan, so the same window is recognisable across tariffs.

*Above cheapest* is what a plan costs you over the winner — the figure to act on.

== When you use electricity, and what it costs

Your average power draw through the day, with the cheapest plan's price windows shaded behind it.

This is the diagnostic chart. A load curve alone tells you when you use power; the shading tells
you whether that timing is expensive. Where the curve runs high inside the red band is where the
bill is made.

The dot marks your daily peak. Seasonal and weekday curves are available from the legend — they
start hidden, because showing seven curves at once is unreadable.

== Where the money goes

The cheapest plan's bill, ranked by size. Bars rather than a pie: six components are hard to
compare by angle and easy by length. The percentage is each component's share of the whole bill.

== Consumption by month

Total energy per calendar month. Part-months at the edges of the window are included as-is, so a
window that starts mid-month shows a short first bar.

== Every half hour

One column per day, one row per half hour, darker meaning more energy. Full resolution — two years
is 35,040 cells and none are averaged away, because the texture is the point: you can see daily
routine, the seasonal shift, and unusual days.

The colour scale is clamped at the 99th percentile. Scaling to the single largest cell would let
one extreme day wash everything else into the palest shade.

== About your data

Findings from reading your file. None of them invalidate a comparison; they explain small
differences against a printed bill. Typical entries:

* *Days carrying fewer intervals than usual.* Spring-forward days legitimately have 46 half hours
  rather than 48, because 2am–3am never happened.
* *Estimated or substituted reads*, where the meter was not actually read.
* *Your heaviest day against your median*, which is irrelevant to a time-of-use comparison but
  decisive for a demand tariff.
