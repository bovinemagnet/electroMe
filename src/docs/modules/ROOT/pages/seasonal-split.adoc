= Seasonal split
:description: Whether being on the best plan for each season beats being on the best plan for the year.

A ranking of annual totals answers "which plan wins over the year". It cannot see a household
whose consumption changes shape with the season, because a year of totals has already thrown that
shape away.

A pool running November to March moves several thousand kilowatt hours into the middle of the
day. A plan that wins on that shape can lose on the winter one, and no amount of staring at an
annual ranking will tell you so — the two effects average out into a single number that favours
whichever plan happens to be a bit cheaper overall. This screen prices every plan over each half
of the year separately, and asks the sharper question: would being on the best plan in each season
beat being on the best plan for the year?

== Getting there

Open `/seasons`, between *Compare* and *What if* in the navigation. The window is the usual
`from`/`to`, and the split itself is `seasonFrom` and `seasonTo`:

----
/seasons?from=2025-07-01&to=2026-06-30&seasonFrom=Nov&seasonTo=Mar
----

`seasonFrom` and `seasonTo` are the first and last month of the first season, inclusive, and the
season is allowed to wrap through December — November to March runs Nov, Dec, Jan, Feb, Mar. A
month can be given as a name, an abbreviation, or a number from 1 to 12, in any case. Left off, or
given a value that means nothing, the split falls back to the default of November to March: a
mistyped URL should show the default report, not an error page. A split covering all twelve
months is rejected, because it would leave no second season to compare against.

== Why the cut falls on a month

The year is cut on month boundaries, not on arbitrary dates, because a tariff's own accumulation
periods are monthly at their shortest. A block rate's allowance, a demand charge's peak window,
a discount's billing cycle — all of them reset on the month. Cut mid-month instead, and you divide
one of those periods across both seasons, and each half gets to start fresh. Both seasons would
then look cheaper than the year they came from, for a reason that has nothing to do with which
plan actually suits the season.

== The answer

The headline has three possible shapes.

*Switching is worth it.* A figure, in pounds, for what two changeovers a year would have saved
over this window, together with which plan wins each season.

*One plan wins both seasons.* The common case, and a more useful finding than any number: it
means the annual ranking was not hiding a seasonal one, and there is nothing to act on.

*Switching would not pay.* The best plan for each season, taken together, does not beat staying
on the best plan for the whole window.

The saving is stated before what it costs to act on it. Two changeovers a year means two sets of
paperwork, two final bills, and any sign-up benefit you can only claim once — weigh the figure
against that, rather than treating it as free money.

== The table

Every plan, cheapest whole-window first, with a column for each season and a column for the
window as a whole. The two season columns normally add up to the whole-window column, which is
what makes a seasonal figure checkable against an annual one — you are not being asked to trust a
number that cannot be verified against the total you already believe.

Normally, but not always. Some tariffs do not decompose that cleanly: a block rate that resets
quarterly gets a fresh allowance in each season rather than sharing the year's one allowance, and
a fixed-amount discount is applied once per costing and so lands in both seasons individually
rather than once across the year. This is measured, not reasoned about — all three figures are
computed independently and compared, so any tariff that fails to decompose says so, including one
nobody anticipated when the screen was built. Such a plan is marked in the table with an asterisk,
and the reason is set out beneath it. Its whole-window figure is still exact; it is only the two
season figures beside it that cannot be added together to check it.

The comparison behind that asterisk is made on unrounded totals, not on the cents shown in the
table. Rounding three figures to cents independently can leave the seasons a cent away from the
year with nothing actually wrong — real accumulation effects run to pounds, not cents, so a
one-cent gap is display rounding rather than a tariff that fails to decompose.
