= Comparing plans
:description: Side by side comparison of two to four tariffs, and whether the answer would survive a change in consumption.

A ranking tells you which plan wins on the consumption you had. It does not tell you *why*, and
it does not tell you how fragile the answer is.

That second point matters more than it looks. Two plans $40 a year apart may be separated by the
daily supply charge alone, in which case the ranking flips the moment you use less. You are
choosing a contract for the next year, not pricing the last one.

== Getting there

Tick two to four plans in xref:browsing-plans.adoc[the plan browser] and press *Compare
selected*, or go straight to a URL:

----
/compare?plans=powershop-tou,vdo-ausnet-2026-27&from=2025-07-01&to=2026-06-30
----

The identifiers are the plan `id` values. A comparison is therefore linkable and reproducible —
the URL fully describes it, and nothing is held in a session.

Two is the minimum, because below that there is nothing to compare. Four is the maximum, because
beyond that the columns stop being readable and the browser is the better tool. Ask for more and
the first four are compared and the rest named.

== The sentence

The screen leads with one sentence per losing plan:

[quote]
____
Powershop time of use is $40.85 cheaper than Victorian Default Offer 2026-27 over this window: $266.37
of that is its lower shoulder, offset by $125.89 more on middle of day, and $72.56 more on
overnight / off-peak, less $27.07 across smaller differences.
____

That sentence is the deliverable. Everything below it on the screen is evidence for it, and a
reader who stops there has not missed anything they needed.

The figures reconcile. Only the three largest reasons are named — more than that stops being a
sentence — so whatever they do not account for is stated as a remainder rather than left for you
to discover by adding them up.

== How fragile the answer is

Under each sentence sits the part a ranking cannot give: whether the winner would still win if
you used more or less.

There are two possible findings.

*One plan wins at every level of use.* The common case, and a stronger result than any number.
It happens whenever one tariff is cheaper on both the standing charge and the rates, so no
amount of consuming more or less can change the order.

*The two swap places at about N kWh a year.* Reported as an annual consumption with your own
usage beside it — "these two swap places at about 8,111 kWh a year, and you used 8,760 kWh over a
comparable period" — because a scale factor means nothing without a reference point. Where your
consumption sits close to the crossing, the figure is highlighted: that is a marginal answer, and
worth knowing before you sign.

[#method]
=== How the crossover is worked out

Your consumption is scaled by a factor and both plans re-costed at each level.

For most tariffs a bill is `standing charge + k x usage`, so the difference between two plans is a
straight line in the scale factor and the crossing has a closed form. That is exact and free, so
it is used wherever it holds — which is flat and time-of-use tariffs, most of the market.

It does not hold for block tariffs, whose thresholds reset per period and do not scale; for demand
charges; or for discounts scoped to part of the bill. For any plan carrying one of those, the
closed form is abandoned and the crossing is found by bisection over full re-costings instead. The
two methods are checked against each other on tariffs where both apply.

WARNING: Both methods scale your consumption *evenly across every half hour*. Real households add
load in particular windows — an electric vehicle charging overnight, a heat pump in the evening —
and a time-of-use tariff prices those differently. Treat the crossover as a guide to how close two
plans are, not as a threshold to plan around.

== The matrix

One row per charge component, one column per plan, using every component any selected plan has.

Rows are components rather than plans because the useful reading is *horizontal*: this plan wins
on supply, that one on the evening peak. The marked cell in each row is the cheapest among the
plans that carry it — and nothing is marked where only one plan has the component, since
"cheapest" among one is not a comparison.

A dash means the plan has no such charge. That is an absence rather than a gap, and it is
information: a flat-rate plan genuinely has no evening rate.

Components use the same vocabulary and the same colours as the rest of the site — overnight,
middle of day, shoulder, evening peak — rather than each plan's own band labels. Two plans whose
evening windows start an hour apart still belong on one row, which is exactly the comparison you
came to make.

A capped band's bill line names its window and, on the block beyond the cap, the allowance too —
`Usage 11:00-15:00 to 50 kWh` rather than `Usage 11:00-15:00`. Classification reads the band
itself rather than that label, so every block of a capped window still lands on one row, instead of
a plan's free block and its evening peak landing on the same row by coincidence of wording.

== The shape chart

Each plan's rate through the day on the left axis, your average draw on the right.

This is the dashboard's load-curve insight extended to several plans at once. Where a plan's cheap
window sits under the high part of your curve is where it saves you money — and that overlap,
rather than the advertised rate, is why two households on one tariff pay different effective
rates.

The two axes are deliberately separate. Rates and power draw share an x-axis and nothing else;
plotting them on one scale would make the comparison meaningless.

== When the selection is not quite right

* *An unknown identifier among several.* The rest are compared and the unknown one named.
  Silently dropping it would let you compare three plans believing you compared four.
* *More than four.* The first four are compared and the surplus named.
* *Fewer than two.* The screen explains and offers the browser, rather than rendering a
  one-column matrix that compares nothing.
